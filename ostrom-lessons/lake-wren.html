Lake Wren is empty

No one here is a villain. Each household did what made sense for itself, because any fish you leave in the water just ends up in someone else's net. Your own restraint changed the ending by a season at most.

Ecologist Garrett Hardin called this the tragedy of the commons (1968). He argued that the usual escapes were private ownership or government control. Elinor Ostrom went and looked at real communities instead: alpine meadows in Switzerland, village forests in Japan, irrigation canals in Spain and the Philippines. Many had shared a resource for centuries with neither. In Governing the Commons (1990) she described eight design principles the long-lasting ones had in common. In 2009 she became the first woman to win the Nobel Prize in economics.

Your job: rebuild Lake Wren. Each mission brings the lake back with one new failure. Pick a fix, watch eight seasons play out, and only then learn the name of the principle you found.

How the lake works

The lake holds at most 100 units of fish. After each season's catch, the fish that remain breed. The lake grows back fastest when it's about half full (up to 15 units a season) and slowly when it's nearly full or nearly empty. Below 15 units, there are too few fish to breed back quickly. Every number in this lesson comes from that one rule, so your choices change the results.
