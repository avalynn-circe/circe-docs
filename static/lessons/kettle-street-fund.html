Twelve houses, one pot

Each month, every household on Kettle Street gets $10 to keep or put in the street fund. Every dollar in the fund buys $3.60 of repairs that all twelve households share. So a dollar you put in comes back to you as 30 cents, and gives 30 cents to each of your eleven neighbors.

The fund is fading, and the textbook was wrong twice

The standard rational-choice prediction is $0 from everyone, every month: each dollar you put in returns only 30 cents to you, so a purely self-interested person keeps it all. Real people don't do that. In hundreds of experiments like this one, people start by giving roughly half. But the hopeful prediction fails too, because giving slides month after month.

In her 1998 presidential address to the American Political Science Association, Elinor Ostrom argued that we need a behavioral theory of the individual to explain both facts: people who are boundedly rational, who learn, who use norms like reciprocity, and who are not all alike.

Your job: figure out who lives on Kettle Street, and what keeps their fund alive. Each mission gives you levers to pull. The name of each idea comes only after you find it.

How Kettle Street works

Each household follows a simple rule. Conditional cooperators give a bit less than they expect others to give, and update that expectation each month from what they saw. Free riders give nothing unless something changes their incentives. Some cooperators are also willing to pay to fine free riders, when fines are allowed. Talking, visible records, an open-ended future and agreed rules change these rules in the ways that experiments by Ostrom and others have reported. The numbers are a simplified teaching model: they follow the direction of the research findings, not their exact values.
