Both plans fail, in opposite ways

The big department is slow at the edges and doesn't know any street well, but it can afford a real crime lab. The block departments know every porch, but nobody is on duty at 3 a.m., and no block can afford a lab. Each plan is good at exactly what the other is bad at.

In the 1960s, reformers called metro areas with dozens of overlapping governments "fragmented" and pushed to merge them. Vincent Ostrom, Charles Tiebout and Robert Warren (1961) argued that many governments could work together as a polycentric system. In the 1970s Elinor Ostrom and her colleagues tested the claim on police, in Indianapolis, Chicago, St. Louis and eventually 80 metro areas, and the evidence did not favor simple consolidation.

Your job: design Larkspur's layers. Each mission brings a new problem. Change the layers, run a year, and see the results. The name of each idea comes only after you find it.

How Larkspur works

Every service in Larkspur can be run at one of four sizes: a single block (64 units), a neighborhood of four blocks (16 units), a district of sixteen blocks (4 units), or the whole metro (1 unit). Each service has a size it does best at, based on the patterns the Ostroms' studies reported: street patrol works best close to residents, and costly specialist work like a crime lab works best at a large scale. The scores are a simplified teaching model. They follow the direction of the research findings, not their exact numbers.
